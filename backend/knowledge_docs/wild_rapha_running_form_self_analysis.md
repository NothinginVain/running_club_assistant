# Fix Your Running Form - Self-Analysis System

## Purpose and limits

This coach-authored guide describes a video-based form check. It can help a runner notice patterns, but video self-analysis cannot diagnose an injury or determine its cause. Pain, repeated injury, or major asymmetry should be assessed by a qualified clinician or running professional.

## Equipment and setup

- Phone and tripod, bottle, or bench
- Usual running shoes
- Straight path of approximately 20-30 m
- Warm-up: 8-10 minutes of easy jogging plus a few strides
- Place the camera near hip height, straight and level
- Avoid an ultra-wide lens unless space requires it
- Use slow motion for the side view when possible
- Keep the whole runner visible in frame

## Recording protocol

1. Run at a normal aerobic effort without deliberately changing form.
2. Record for at least one minute in running shoes.
3. Remove the shoes and repeat from exactly the same camera position.
4. Capture all three angles before comparing the recordings.

The source suggests `180 - age` as a rough starting estimate for maximum aerobic heart rate. This formula is approximate and should not be treated as an individualized threshold test.

## Camera angles

| Angle | Main checks |
| --- | --- |
| Side | Cadence, overstriding, foot strike, arm action, shoulders, head and torso posture |
| Back | Knee alignment and hip drop |
| Front | Arm crossover and head/torso alignment |

## Seven-point checklist

### 1. Cadence

Cadence is steps per minute. It can be obtained from a running watch or estimated by tapping along with at least 10-15 foot strikes in recorded footage and averaging several attempts.

Compare the shod and barefoot recordings. A higher barefoot cadence may accompany shorter steps, softer landings, and less overstriding. The guide emphasizes observation rather than forcing a universal cadence target.

### 2. Foot strike and overstriding

From the side, pause at initial ground contact and imagine a vertical line below the hips. Look for:

- Foot landing far ahead of the hips
- A straight knee at contact
- Loud or heavy impact
- Strong dorsiflexion, with toes pulled upward

The guide distinguishes overstriding from heel striking: heel contact is not automatically harmful, and forcing a forefoot strike may overload the Achilles tendon. The main observation is whether contact occurs far ahead of the body’s center of mass.

### 3. Knee alignment

From the rear, pause during stance and observe whether the knee tracks over the foot or moves inward. Repeated inward collapse is described as knee valgus. Compare both sides rather than interpreting one frame in isolation.

Suggested cue from the source: push the ground back and slightly outward, without exaggeration.

### 4. Hip drop

From the rear, pause while one foot is supporting the body. Observe whether the opposite side of the pelvis drops and whether the torso leans to compensate. A small amount can be normal; repeated or marked asymmetry may warrant professional assessment.

Suggested cue: stay tall over the stance leg.

### 5. Arm crossover

From the front, check whether the hands travel forward and backward or cross the body’s midline. Also look for flared elbows and excessive torso rotation.

Suggested cue: swing back, not across.

### 6. Shoulder rotation

From the front, observe whether the shoulders remain relatively square or rotate markedly from side to side. Some rotation is natural. Look for dramatic chest twisting, one shoulder pulling far behind the other, or the head moving laterally.

Suggested cue: run tall with quiet shoulders.

### 7. Head and torso posture

From the side, observe whether the head is stacked over the shoulders and whether forward lean comes mainly from the ankles rather than folding at the waist. Look for a forward-jutting chin, rounded upper back, collapsed chest, or constantly downward gaze.

Suggested cue: tall and light.

## How to use the observations

- Compare shod and barefoot recordings under the same conditions.
- Compare left and right sides and repeat the recording when rested and when fatigued.
- Change one cue at a time rather than attempting to correct every feature simultaneously.
- Stop barefoot running if it causes pain; barefoot filming is an observation method, not a required training prescription.
