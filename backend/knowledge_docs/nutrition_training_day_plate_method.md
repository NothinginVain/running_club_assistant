# Training-Day Plate Method for Runners

Meal composition should respond to the training load. A harder or longer day generally requires more carbohydrate than a moderate day because carbohydrate supports glycogen availability and higher-intensity exercise.

## Plate comparison

| Component | Moderate training day | Hard or high-volume training day |
|---|---:|---:|
| Vegetables and fruit | About 40% | About 25% |
| Carbohydrate | About 30% | About 50% |
| Protein | About 30% | About 25% |
| Healthy fats | Add a suitable portion | Add a suitable portion |

These percentages are visual planning guides, not precise medical prescriptions.

## Moderate training day

Use a balanced plate that supports health, recovery, and ordinary training:

- vegetables and fruit for micronutrients and fibre;
- complex carbohydrates such as rice, pasta, oats, potatoes, or bread;
- protein such as tofu, beans, lentils, eggs, dairy, soy foods, fish, or meat;
- healthy fats such as nuts, seeds, olive oil, or avocado.

## Hard or high-volume training day

Increase the carbohydrate share to support greater energy demand and glycogen replacement. Keep protein, vegetables, and healthy fats present rather than building the meal from carbohydrate alone.

## Practical rule

Do not think in terms of carbohydrate-free days. Think in terms of baseline carbohydrate days and carbohydrate top-up days, matched to training volume and intensity.

## Safety boundary

Individual needs vary with body size, total training, health, food access, gastrointestinal tolerance, and performance goals. Athletes with medical conditions or signs of underfueling should seek individualized professional guidance.
